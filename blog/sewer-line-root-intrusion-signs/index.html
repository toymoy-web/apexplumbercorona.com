Signs Tree Roots Have Gotten Into Your Sewer Line

Root intrusion is one of the more common, and more misunderstood, causes of a recurring drain problem in older Corona neighborhoods with aging clay or cast iron sewer laterals.

Call Now

Many of Corona's older neighborhoods still run original clay or cast iron sewer laterals, and decades of established landscaping near those lines makes root intrusion a real, ongoing risk. It's also one of the more misunderstood plumbing problems, since the symptoms often look like an ordinary clog at first.

How Roots Actually Get In

Tree and shrub roots are drawn toward the moisture and nutrients inside a sewer line, and they find their way in through small cracks, loose joints, or connection points, especially in older clay or cast iron pipe. Once a root gets a foothold, it grows along the interior of the pipe, gradually catching debris and narrowing the flow path further over time.

The Clearest Warning Sign: Clogs That Keep Coming Back

A single clog cleared by snaking and never seen again is normal. A clog that returns within weeks, especially in the same drain or affecting multiple fixtures at once, is a strong sign the problem isn't just debris, it's something inside the line itself narrowing the flow repeatedly.

Other Signs Worth Watching For

Gurgling sounds from drains when other fixtures are used, slow drainage across multiple fixtures rather than just one, and sewage odor in the yard near where the line runs are all additional signs consistent with root intrusion. An unusually lush, green patch of grass along the line's path can also indicate a partial break that's inadvertently fertilizing the soil above it.

How This Gets Confirmed

A video camera inspection is the only way to know for certain. Run through the line, it shows root intrusion directly, along with its location and severity, rather than leaving you to guess based on symptoms alone. This also distinguishes root intrusion from other possible causes, like a bellied section of pipe or a straightforward blockage, which need different fixes entirely and shouldn't be treated the same way just because the symptom looks similar on the surface.

How Root Intrusion Actually Gets Fixed

For moderate intrusion without significant structural damage, hydro jetting can clear roots from the interior of the pipe, sometimes combined with a root-cutting attachment for tougher growth. This addresses the immediate blockage but doesn't prevent roots from returning, since the crack or joint that let them in the first time is still there. For a more permanent fix, trenchless pipe lining or, in more severe cases, full replacement of the affected section addresses the underlying entry point rather than just the symptom. Any repair work follows standard UPC plumbing code practices for the pipe material and application involved.

Will Removing The Tree Solve It?

Removing the specific tree stops that root source, but it doesn't repair whatever crack or gap let the roots in to begin with, and roots from a neighboring tree could eventually do the same thing. It also doesn't undo intrusion that's already happened. Removing the tree is sometimes part of a complete solution, but it isn't a substitute for actually repairing the line.

Can This Be Prevented?

For a line that hasn't had root intrusion yet, being thoughtful about where new trees and large shrubs are planted relative to the sewer line's known path reduces future risk. For an existing line with a documented history of root problems, periodic camera inspections can catch new intrusion early, before it grows into a full blockage.

Which Trees Are The Biggest Risk

Fast-growing species with aggressive, water-seeking root systems, common ornamental and shade trees among them, pose more risk than slower-growing or deeper-rooted varieties. This doesn't mean removing every tree near a sewer line, but it's worth factoring into decisions about new landscaping, especially directly above or immediately adjacent to a known line path.

Does This Affect Newer Homes Too?

Root intrusion is far more common in older clay and cast iron lines because their joints are more prone to the small gaps roots exploit. Newer PVC sewer lines use fewer joints and tighter seals, making root intrusion meaningfully less common, though not entirely impossible if a line is damaged by other means first, such as ground settling or nearby excavation work.

How Corona's Older Neighborhoods Compare

Central Corona's original 1960s and 1970s tract housing runs the same era of sewer infrastructure as the homes themselves, meaning clay or cast iron laterals are common throughout these neighborhoods. Combined with decades of established landscaping, this is exactly the combination that makes root intrusion a recurring, real issue here rather than a rare edge case worth dismissing.

What A Camera Inspection Visit Actually Involves

A technician feeds a video camera on a flexible cable through an accessible cleanout, watching the footage in real time on a monitor as it travels the length of the line. The visit typically takes under an hour for a standard residential line. You get to see exactly what the camera sees, root intrusion, cracks, or a clean line, rather than taking someone's word for a diagnosis you can't verify yourself, which matters most when the recommendation involves a significant repair cost.

The Bottom Line

A drain that clogs once and stays clear is nothing to worry about. One that keeps coming back, especially in an older Corona home with an original clay or cast iron lateral, is worth a camera inspection rather than repeated snaking visits. Those visits only address the symptom each time without ever resolving what's actually causing it underground, which means the same call and the same cost eventually happen again, often sooner than expected.
