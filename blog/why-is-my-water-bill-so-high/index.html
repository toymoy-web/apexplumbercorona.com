Why Is My Water Bill Suddenly So High?

A sharp, unexplained increase in your water bill is one of the most reliable early signs of a hidden leak, before any visible symptom shows up.

Talk to a plumber.
(951) 467-1450
Call Now

A water bill that jumps noticeably with no real change in your household's habits is one of the most common ways a hidden plumbing leak first gets noticed, often before any visible sign shows up. Understanding what usually causes this can help you narrow things down before calling anyone.

The First Step: Confirm It's Actually A Leak

Before assuming a leak, shut off every fixture and appliance that uses water, then check your water meter. If the meter's flow indicator is still moving with everything off, water is going somewhere it shouldn't. This simple test rules out billing errors or seasonal usage changes and confirms you're dealing with a real leak somewhere in the system.

Toilet Leaks Are More Common Than People Think

A worn toilet flapper that doesn't seal completely lets water continuously trickle from the tank into the bowl, often silently enough that it's never noticed by sound alone. This is one of the single most common causes of an unexplained water bill increase. A simple check: add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking.

Irrigation System Leaks

If you have a sprinkler system, a cracked line or a valve that doesn't fully close can waste a significant amount of water without any visible sign on the surface, especially for lines buried underground. This is worth checking, particularly if the increase coincided with recent yard work or irrigation system changes.

Slab Leaks

For a home on a concrete slab foundation, a leak in a supply line running underneath it often shows up as a rising water bill well before any other symptom, since the water is largely hidden from view. This is especially relevant in Corona's older neighborhoods with original 1960s and 1970s copper plumbing under the slab.

Water Heater Leaks

A slow leak from the tank itself, a loose fitting, or a failing pressure relief valve can waste water continuously without an obvious puddle, particularly if the water heater drains to a floor drain rather than pooling visibly. Checking around the base of the unit for dampness is a quick way to rule this in or out.

Pool Leaks, If You Have One

A leak in a pool's plumbing loop or shell shows up on the house water bill if the pool has an automatic fill system compensating for the loss. The bucket test, comparing water loss in the pool against a bucket over 24 hours, is the standard way to confirm whether a pool is genuinely losing more water than normal evaporation accounts for.

Faucet And Fixture Drips

A single dripping faucet seems minor, but a worn cartridge or washer that drips continuously can waste a real amount of water over a full month, especially across multiple fixtures at once if several are due for the same repair. This is one of the easier causes to rule in or out with a quick walkthrough of the house checking every faucet and showerhead for a slow drip.

What If You Can't Find It Yourself?

If the meter test confirms a leak but none of the common causes above turn up anything obvious, that's exactly the situation professional leak detection is built for. Acoustic and electronic equipment can locate a leak in a supply line, whether it's under a slab, behind a wall, or underground between the meter and the house, without any guesswork or unnecessary digging.

How Much Water Loss Are We Actually Talking About

Even a small, continuous leak adds up over a full billing cycle. A toilet flapper leaking at a moderate rate can waste a meaningful amount of water per day, which compounds into a significant increase by the time a monthly bill arrives. This is part of why catching it early, rather than assuming a one-time billing anomaly, saves real money over time.

Ruling Out A Billing Error

Before assuming a leak, it's worth briefly considering whether a billing error explains the jump, an estimated read rather than an actual meter read, or a rate change your utility implemented. Comparing your current bill's usage figure against your own meter reading is a quick way to confirm the number itself is accurate before spending time hunting for a leak that may not exist.

Comparing To Past Bills The Right Way

Look at the same billing period from a year earlier rather than just the previous month, since seasonal factors like irrigation use genuinely do shift normal usage throughout the year. A jump that's unusual compared to the same month last year is more meaningful than a jump compared to a lower-usage winter month.

What A Corona-Area Utility Bill Can Tell You

Many water utilities, including the Corona Department of Water & Power, provide usage history and sometimes leak alerts based on continuous flow patterns detected at the meter. Checking your utility's online portal for this data, if available, can sometimes confirm a leak's existence and even a rough timeline before you do any testing yourself.

The Bottom Line

A sudden, unexplained jump in your water bill is rarely random. Working through the common causes above, starting with the simple meter test, usually points you in the right direction, and a professional detection visit can confirm what you can't verify yourself, often the same day you call.
