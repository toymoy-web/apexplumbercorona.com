Signs You Might Have a Slab Leak

Slab leaks rarely announce themselves clearly. Here's what to actually look and listen for in a Corona-area home.

Talk to a plumber.
(951) 467-1450
Call Now

If your home sits on a concrete slab foundation, which is true for most houses in Corona built from the 1960s through the 1980s, a slab leak is one of the more disruptive plumbing problems you can run into. The tricky part is that slab leaks rarely show up as an obvious puddle. They tend to announce themselves through smaller, easier-to-miss signs first.

1. A Warm Spot On The Floor

If your home's hot water supply line runs under the slab and develops a leak, the escaping hot water warms the concrete directly above it. Walk barefoot across your tile or hardwood floors and pay attention to any spot that feels noticeably warmer than the rest, especially if it's not near a heating vent or in direct sun. This is one of the clearest physical signs of a slab leak, and it only applies to leaks on the hot water side of the system.

2. A Water Bill That Doesn't Match Your Usage

A slab leak runs continuously, day and night, whether or not anyone is home. That steady, invisible water loss often shows up on your bill well before any visible sign appears. If your usage jumps noticeably and you haven't changed your habits, added a new fixture, or started watering the lawn more, a hidden leak is worth ruling out. A simple check: shut off every fixture in the house and see if your water meter is still moving. If it is, water is going somewhere.

3. Water Or Dampness Near The Foundation Edge

Water from a slab leak doesn't always come straight up through the floor. It can travel sideways along the path of least resistance and surface at the edge of the slab, sometimes showing up as unexplained moisture along a garage wall or where the foundation meets the exterior siding, even with no rain in the forecast.

4. Reduced Water Pressure At Specific Fixtures

If a slab leak is drawing pressure away from the line before it reaches certain fixtures, you might notice weaker flow at one sink or shower while the rest of the house seems normal. This is a subtler sign, and it's easy to mistake for a fixture-specific problem like a clogged aerator, but it's worth mentioning if a plumber is already out for something else.

5. The Sound Of Running Water With Everything Off

Some people report hearing a faint hissing or trickling sound, particularly at night when the house is otherwise quiet, even though no fixture is running. This is less common than the other signs but worth paying attention to if you notice it.

Why This Comes Up So Often In Corona

A meaningful share of Corona's housing stock was built in the 1960s and 1970s, when copper supply lines were routed directly under the concrete slab rather than through interior walls. Fifty-plus years underground, even good copper eventually develops pinhole leaks from soil chemistry, minor ground movement, and simple age. That's a big part of why slab leak detection is one of the more common calls we get from homeowners in central Corona's older neighborhoods specifically, as opposed to newer developments built with modern plumbing.

What To Do If You Notice One Of These Signs

You don't need to diagnose the exact cause before calling. Acoustic and thermal detection equipment can locate a slab leak without any guesswork and without opening up your floor first. Any repair that follows detection is performed to standard UPC plumbing code practices for the pipe material and repair method involved. The sooner it's confirmed, the less time water has to sit under your flooring, which matters both for your home and for keeping any related repair as straightforward as possible.

How A Slab Leak Actually Gets Confirmed

A proper diagnosis usually combines a few methods rather than relying on just one. Acoustic ground microphones pick up the specific sound of pressurized water escaping a line under concrete, which narrows the search to a fairly small area. A thermal imaging camera can then confirm a hot water leak by reading the temperature difference on the surface directly above it. For cases where the picture still isn't clear, isolating sections of the plumbing system and testing pressure can determine which specific line, hot or cold, is actually losing water. None of this requires breaking concrete first.

Repair Options Once A Slab Leak Is Confirmed

There are generally two paths once a leak is located. Direct access means opening the concrete at the confirmed leak point and repairing that specific section, which tends to be the faster option for a single, well-isolated leak. The alternative is rerouting, where the damaged section of pipe is abandoned in place and a new line is run through the walls or attic instead, avoiding concrete work entirely. Rerouting often makes more sense on an older home where the rest of the plumbing system is also aging, since it sidesteps the possibility of finding a second or third leak in the same slab down the road.

Should You Worry About Mold?

A slab leak that's caught within a reasonable window, generally measured in days rather than weeks, is unlikely to cause significant mold growth, since the water is largely contained under a hard surface rather than soaking into porous material. The bigger risk comes from a leak that goes unnoticed for an extended period, especially under carpet or engineered flooring that holds moisture. If you've had a warm spot or unexplained water bill for what you suspect has been a while, it's worth mentioning that timeline when you call so a technician can check the surrounding flooring condition along with the leak itself.

Is A Slab Leak Covered By Homeowner's Insurance?

This depends on the specific cause and your policy's language, but the general pattern is that a sudden, accidental leak is more likely to be covered than gradual, long-term seepage. A pinhole leak that developed slowly over months or years due to normal pipe aging is often treated differently than a sudden pipe failure. Either way, proper documentation, photos, moisture readings, and a written scope of the repair, gives you what you need to make your case to an insurer, even if the final coverage decision isn't guaranteed either way.

The Bottom Line

A warm floor spot, a water bill that doesn't add up, or dampness near your foundation are all worth taking seriously, especially in an older Corona home with original copper plumbing. Catching a slab leak early keeps the eventual repair simpler and limits the water damage risk that comes with letting it run unnoticed. If any of these signs sound familiar, a proper detection visit is the fastest way to get a real answer instead of guessing.
