Pool Leak Or Just Evaporation? How To Tell The Difference

Every pool loses some water to evaporation. The question is how much is normal, and the bucket test gives you a real answer.

Talk to a plumber.
(951) 467-1450
Call Now

Every backyard pool loses water to evaporation, especially in a warm, dry climate like Corona's. The tricky part is knowing whether the water level drop you're seeing is normal evaporation or an actual leak somewhere in the plumbing, shell, or equipment. The good news is there's a simple, genuinely reliable test you can run yourself before deciding whether to call anyone.

The Bucket Test, Step By Step

Place a bucket on the pool's top step so it's partially submerged, with the water level inside the bucket matching the pool's water level. Mark both levels, the water level in the bucket and the water level in the pool, with tape or a marker. Turn off the pool's auto-fill if it has one, and let the pool run normally, including the pump on its regular cycle, for a full 24 hours. After that period, compare how much the level dropped in the bucket versus the pool.

What The Results Mean

If both levels dropped roughly the same amount, that water loss is evaporation, and it's normal, not a leak. If the pool's level dropped noticeably more than the bucket's, that difference points to an actual leak somewhere in the system, since the bucket experiences the same evaporation conditions but isn't affected by a plumbing or shell leak.

Why This Test Actually Works

Evaporation rates depend on temperature, humidity, and wind, all factors that affect the bucket and the pool equally since they're sitting in the same environment. A genuine leak, by contrast, only affects the pool, since it's a loss of water through a specific point in the plumbing or structure that has nothing to do with weather conditions. Isolating for evaporation this way is what makes the comparison meaningful.

Normal Evaporation Rates To Expect

In Corona's climate, it's normal for a pool to lose roughly a quarter inch to half an inch of water per day during hot, dry, or windy conditions, more in peak summer heat. If your pool is dropping significantly beyond that range, or if the bucket test confirms a real difference, that's a reasonable point to stop guessing and get an actual detection visit.

Common Real Leak Sources

When it is a genuine leak, the cause usually falls into one of a few categories. Underground plumbing lines between the pool and the equipment pad can develop leaks as the original pipe ages, particularly in older pools built alongside 1970s-era homes. Cracks near skimmers, light niches, or return fittings in the shell itself are another common source. Equipment-side leaks, at the pump, filter, or heater, are also common and are sometimes mistaken for a pool leak when the actual source is the equipment itself rather than the pool structure or its underground plumbing.

Can You Narrow It Down Further Yourself?

A few visual checks can help before calling. Damp or unusually green grass near the equipment pad often points to an equipment-side or underground line leak in that area. Cracks visible around lights, skimmers, or the shell itself are worth photographing and noting. None of this replaces professional pressure and dye testing, but it can help a technician start the visit with useful context rather than from zero.

Do You Need To Drain The Pool To Find It?

Usually not. Pressure testing individual lines and dye testing at suspected points can identify most leaks without draining the pool at all. Draining is typically only necessary for the repair itself in certain cases, not for the detection process.

How Detection Actually Confirms A Leak

A proper detection visit combines a few methods rather than relying on a single test. Each line, skimmer, main drain, and return, is pressure tested individually to isolate exactly which one is losing water. Dye testing at suspected cracks, fittings, or light niches shows visibly where water is escaping the shell. The equipment pad itself gets checked directly too, since a surprising number of suspected pool leaks turn out to be equipment-side rather than the pool structure. This work follows standard plumbing practices for the pipe material and fittings found.

Seasonal Factors Worth Knowing

Evaporation rates climb noticeably during Corona's hottest, driest months, and wind exposure matters too, a pool in an open, breezy yard evaporates faster than one shielded by fencing or landscaping. If you're comparing your pool's water loss against a general rule of thumb, keep in mind that summer conditions here can push normal evaporation toward the higher end of typical ranges, which is exactly why the bucket test matters more than a fixed number.

What If The Bucket Test Is Inconclusive?

Sometimes the difference between the pool and bucket levels is small enough that it's genuinely hard to tell from a single 24-hour test. Running the test again over a longer period, 48 to 72 hours, can make a marginal difference more obvious. If it's still unclear after that, a professional detection visit is a reasonable next step rather than continuing to guess, since pressure and dye testing don't depend on interpreting a borderline water-level comparison the way a home test does.

The Bottom Line

The bucket test takes a day and costs nothing, and it's the fastest way to rule evaporation in or out before assuming the worst. If it confirms a real leak, a proper detection visit with pressure and dye testing will find the actual source without draining the pool first or guessing at a fix based on incomplete information, saving both water and money in the long run.
