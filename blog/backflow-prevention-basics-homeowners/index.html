Backflow Prevention Basics For Homeowners With Irrigation

If your home has a sprinkler system, a backflow device probably applies to you. Here's what it does, why annual testing matters, and what happens if you skip it.

(951) 467-1450
Call Now

If your home has a lawn irrigation system, there's a good chance you already have a backflow prevention device, whether or not you've ever thought about it. Understanding what it does and why annual testing matters helps you stay compliant without treating it as a mysterious requirement someone is trying to upsell you on.

What Backflow Actually Means

Backflow is water flowing in the reverse direction from normal, potentially pulling non-potable water back into your home's drinking water supply. This can happen during a sudden drop in water pressure, such as a water main break or heavy firefighting draw nearby, which can create suction that pulls water backward through a connected system like irrigation.

Why Irrigation Systems Specifically

An irrigation system connects directly to your home's potable water supply. The water sitting in sprinkler lines and in contact with soil, fertilizer, or pesticide residue is exactly the kind of non-potable water a backflow event could pull back into your drinking water. A backflow prevention device installed at the connection point stops that reverse flow from happening.

Is This Actually Required?

Most California water agencies, including those serving the Corona area, require backflow prevention devices on irrigation connections as part of standard cross-connection control programs. Many also require annual testing to confirm the device is still functioning correctly. This isn't unique to Corona. It's standard practice across most regulated water systems, but it's easy to forget about once the device is installed and working.

What Annual Testing Actually Involves

A certified tester checks the device's internal components to confirm they're sealing and functioning as designed. This is a relatively quick visit for a standard residential device, and if it passes, you get documentation to keep on file or submit to your water provider, depending on their specific process.

What Happens If A Device Fails Testing

A failed test usually points to a worn internal component, most often a check valve or seal, which is frequently repairable rather than requiring full device replacement. The technician performing the test can typically diagnose this on the spot and give you a straightforward repair option before the device goes back into service.

What If You Don't Have Irrigation?

Backflow prevention isn't only about irrigation. Pool and spa fill lines, certain commercial connections, and irrigation systems that inject fertilizer directly into the water line all carry their own cross-connection risks and may require a device even without a standard sprinkler system. If you're not sure whether your specific setup requires one, that's a reasonable question to ask directly rather than assume either way.

Can You Test It Yourself?

No. Backflow testing requires a certified tester using calibrated equipment to produce results that are accepted by water authorities. A device that looks fine visually can still fail internally in ways that aren't obvious without the proper test equipment, which is exactly why certification and calibrated testing exist as a requirement rather than a formality.

What Happens If You Skip Testing

Consequences vary by water agency, but repeated non-compliance can result in a warning, a fine, or in some cases a restriction on water service until the device is tested and confirmed functional. Beyond the compliance angle, a genuinely failed device does carry the real cross-connection risk the whole system is designed to prevent, which is the actual reason the requirement exists in the first place.

Types Of Backflow Devices

Residential irrigation systems commonly use either a pressure vacuum breaker or a double check valve assembly, depending on the specific setup and local requirements. Commercial properties and higher-hazard connections, like those with chemical injection for fertilizer, often require a more robust reduced pressure principle device. Which type applies to your property is determined by your water agency's requirements for that connection type, not a matter of personal preference.

New Installation Considerations

If your property doesn't have a device yet and you're adding irrigation for the first time, installation is typically a contained job at the point where the irrigation line splits off from your main water supply. The specific device required depends on your local water agency's classification of the connection, which is worth confirming before installation rather than assuming any device will satisfy the requirement.

What To Do Before Your Annual Test Is Due

  • Check your records or your water provider's portal for your device's last test date if you're not sure when it's due.
  • Locate the device itself so you can point a tester to it quickly rather than searching during the visit.
  • Note any visible leaking, corrosion, or damage around the device beforehand so you can mention it to the tester.
  • Schedule testing before the deadline rather than after, since some agencies flag or restrict service for lapsed testing.

Keeping a simple annual reminder, tied to the same month each year, is the easiest way to avoid letting testing lapse without noticing. It's the kind of routine maintenance item that's genuinely easy to forget between visits, especially once the device has been working reliably for several years in a row without any apparent issues.

The Bottom Line

If you have irrigation, a pool fill line, or certain other connections, a backflow device and annual testing are likely already part of your compliance requirements, whether or not you've been keeping track of them. Staying current is a small, routine cost against a real cross-connection risk and the compliance issues that come with ignoring it year after year until something eventually forces the issue.
