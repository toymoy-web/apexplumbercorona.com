Common Issues.

What Usually Brings Us Out

A Toilet That Runs Constantly

Almost always a worn flapper or fill valve, an inexpensive part that wastes real water when left unfixed.

Repeated Clogging

A toilet that clogs often despite normal use can point to a partial blockage further down the line, not the toilet itself.

Rocking Or A Leak At The Base

A wax ring that has failed or a cracked flange lets water seep out at the floor, which can damage subflooring if left alone.

Weak Flush

Mineral buildup in the rim jets or an aging flush valve both reduce flush performance over time.

Overflowing Now.

A Toilet That Is Overflowing Or Backing Up

Water rising in the bowl is an emergency until it stops. Reach behind the toilet, turn the supply valve clockwise, and lift the tank lid if the fill keeps running.

The next question is whether it is one toilet or the whole house. If a tub or floor drain also gurgles, the blockage is probably in the main line. That is a different job, covered on our sewer line repair page and our drain cleaning page.

If only this toilet is affected, a local clog or a worn part is the likely cause, and the repair is usually simple.

Why This Matters In Corona.

Older Fixtures And Hard Water

Many Corona homes built before the mid-1990s still run original toilets that use far more water per flush than current models require. Corona's moderately hard water also accelerates mineral buildup inside the tank and rim jets on any toilet, old or new. A toilet upgraded to a modern low-flow model both reduces water use and tends to clog less often than an aging unit with a weakening flush.

Repair Or Replace.

When Each Makes Sense

Repair Usually Fits When

The bowl and tank are in good condition and the issue is a specific part like a flapper, fill valve, or wax ring.

Replacement Usually Fits When

The toilet is old enough that parts are hard to find, it's cracked, or you want a higher-efficiency model to cut water use.

Wax Ring And Flange Work

A rocking toilet or a leak at the base is a wax ring or flange issue, resolved by pulling and resetting the toilet properly.

New Installation

Whether replacing an old unit or adding a new bathroom, installation includes proper flange height and a new wax seal every time. We also check that the water supply line and shutoff valve are in good condition before setting the new toilet, since replacing those later means pulling the toilet again.

Toilet Running Nonstop?

That wastes real water every day it goes unfixed, and it's usually a quick repair.

Not Sure If It's Worth Fixing.

A Quick Way To Tell

If the toilet is under 10-15 years old and the issue is a specific part, repair is almost always the better value. If it's original to an older home, cracked, or this is a repeat repair, replacement with a modern low-flow model usually pays for itself over time in both water savings and fewer service calls.

If Water Is Overflowing.

What To Do Before We Arrive

Shut Off The Toilet's Own Valve First

The small valve behind the toilet, turned clockwise, stops water to that fixture without affecting the rest of the house.

Lift The Tank Lid If It Won't Stop Running

Reaching in to lift the flapper or float can stop an active overflow while you wait, if you're comfortable doing so.

Avoid Repeated Flushing

Flushing again on top of standing water pushes more water onto the floor rather than clearing anything.

Mop Up Standing Water Promptly

Water sitting on bathroom flooring for an extended period raises the risk of damage to subflooring below.

Common Parts.

Fill Valves, Supply Valves, Flappers And Wax Rings

Fill Valve

A tank that hisses, refills on its own or never fills often needs a new fill valve.

Supply Valve

A valve under the tank that drips or will not close needs replacing so the toilet can be isolated.

Flapper

A running toilet is often a worn flapper that no longer seals the tank.

Wax Ring

Water around the base or a rocking bowl usually means the seal has failed.

A cracked bowl or tank is different. Porcelain cannot be repaired, so the answer is replacement. If the fixture is just old and wasteful, our section above explains when replacing beats repairing.

FAQs.

Common Questions

Is a running toilet really worth fixing quickly?

Yes. A running toilet can waste a meaningful amount of water per day, and the fix itself is usually inexpensive.

Do you supply the toilet, or do I?

Either works. We can recommend and supply a model, or install one you've already purchased.

Why does my toilet clog so often?

If it's not just old age or low flush power, we check further down the line, since a partial blockage there can mimic a weak-flush symptom.

How long does installation take?

Most single-toilet installations, including removing the old unit, are completed in under two hours.

Do older toilets actually use that much more water?

Yes. Pre-1994 toilets often use 3.5 to 7 gallons per flush, compared to 1.28 gallons or less for a current low-flow model, which adds up meaningfully over a year of normal household use.

Why is my toilet backing up and not flushing?

A clog in the trap is the usual cause. If other drains in the house also slow down, the blockage may be farther down the line.

Can you replace a toilet supply valve or a fill valve?

Yes. Both are small repairs and a good time to replace worn parts, so the toilet can be shut off cleanly next time.

Is a cracked toilet tank or bowl repairable?

No. Cracks in porcelain do not hold a lasting repair. Replacement is the safe route, and we can handle the install too.

My toilet clog will not clear. Is it the main line?

If only one toilet is affected, the clog is local. If several fixtures are slow or a tub gurgles when the toilet flushes, the main line is the likely culprit.

Local Neighborhoods.

Toilets In Corona's Older And Newer Homes

Homes from the 1980s and early 1990s, which includes much of Sierra del Oro, may still have toilets from before low-flow standards arrived. Newer South Corona homes around Dos Lagos have modern fixtures but the same hard water, which scales the fill valve and rim jets. Either way, the fix starts with finding out whether the problem is the flush, the fill, or the seal.
